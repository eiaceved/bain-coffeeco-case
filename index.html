01

How this case works

This is one of Bain's public sample cases. It's short and focused on two classic skills: sizing a market and working out a breakeven point. Then you turn the numbers into advice.

Bain's sample cases show a "good answer" and a "better answer." Here, the good answer does the math correctly. The better answer asks whether the result is realistic, and what else could make or break the shop.

Two ways to size a market

Bain's case names both

  • Top-down: start with a big number, like the population, and narrow it to the people who'd buy.
  • Bottom-up: start small, like what one shop or one person does, and scale it up.
  • Doing both and comparing them is a strong sanity check.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Top-down sizing

Start with a large total, like everyone in the city, and narrow it step by step.

Bottom-up sizing

Start with one unit, like one shop's sales in a day, and build up.

Breakeven

The point where revenue exactly covers costs. Revenue minus costs equals zero.

Contribution margin

What each sale leaves after its own cost. A £3 coffee that costs £1 to make leaves £2.

Fixed costs

Costs that don't change with sales, like rent and staff.

Variable costs

Costs that rise with every sale, like beans, milk, and cups.

Opening cost

A one-time cost to get started, like the fit-out and the espresso machine.

Payback period

How long it takes profits to earn back the opening cost.

Market share

The shop's sales as a share of the whole market's sales.

Go-to-market

How a business will win customers: where it opens, what it offers, and why people choose it.

She thinks there's a real opportunity, and she wants to know: is opening a coffee shop in Cambridge a good idea?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So my friend wants to know whether opening one coffee shop in Cambridge is a good idea, meaning whether there's enough demand and whether she can sell enough to make money. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." Defining "good idea" as enough demand plus a path to profit gives you something you can actually test.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with practice answers

  • Who are the target customers: residents, students, tourists?
    She wants to focus on Cambridge residents only. That's from Bain's case.
  • What does success look like for her?
    Running the shop for at least five years and earning back the money she puts in.
  • What will she charge?
    About £3 a cup.
  • Who's the competition?
    A few big chain cafés and lots of small independent shops.

Apart from the residents-only focus, these answers were written for practice. The second question matters most: how long she plans to run the shop changes how you judge the opening cost.

Build the framework

Interviewer

How would you structure this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds. Bain's case suggests three areas to cover: the size of the opportunity, the cost of opening a shop, and how she'll win customers.

Say it like this

"I'd look at three things. First, the size of the opportunity: how many cups of coffee Cambridge residents buy from shops each year. Second, the costs: what it takes to open and run the shop, and how many cups she'd need to sell to break even. Third, go-to-market: who she's up against and how she'd win customers. If breakeven is a small share of the market and she has a way to stand out, it's a good idea. I'd start by sizing the market."

Why it works

  • It matches the three areas Bain's case suggests.
  • It states the decision rule up front: a small share needed, plus a way to win.
  • It connects the buckets: market size becomes the yardstick for breakeven.

05

Size the market

Interviewer

Let's size the opportunity. Focus on Cambridge residents. Assume about 100,000 residents, and that a coffee drinker has about 1 cup a day. How many cups do residents buy from coffee shops in a year?

Your move

Go top-down. Start with residents, narrow to people who buy coffee from shops, then multiply by cups a day and days a year. Say each assumption out loud. Any reasonable assumption is fine.

Numbers from Bain's case. Bain's breakeven counts the full opening cost in year one.

Your move

Write the equation first: 3 times cups, minus all the costs, equals zero. Then solve, and compare the answer with the market size.

  1. 1 · The equation3Q − (£163,740 + £245,610 + £1 × Q) = 0Q = ?
  2. 2 · Margin per cup£3 − £1£2
  3. 3 · Costs to cover£163,740 + £245,610£409,350
  4. 4 · Breakeven£409,350 ÷ £2204,675
  5. 5 · Market share needed204,675 ÷ 7,000,000≈3%
  6. 6 · Cups per day204,675 ÷ 365. Almost 1 a minute for 10 hours≈560

Quick check: how many cups a year cover just the running costs, leaving the opening cost aside?

163,740 pounds divided by 2 pounds a cup is 81,870 cups, about 225 a day. Everyday version: if a lemonade stand's weekly costs are 20 dollars and each cup leaves 2 dollars, it needs 10 cups a week to cover them, before paying back the stand itself.

How would she win customers?

Interviewer

Say the numbers work. How should she compete? Brainstorm first, then score these six ideas for ease and impact.

Your move

Bain's case raises three questions: how she'll stand out, whether one big player dominates, and whether the market is full of small shops she could beat. Brainstorm for 60 seconds with those in mind.

Then

Give one reason for each score, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "Location matters most: she needs hundreds of cups a day, so she has to be where people walk past. Then a simple loyalty card to bring regulars back, and a space people want to stay in. I'd avoid undercutting the chains, because a lower price shrinks the 2 pound margin and pushes breakeven even higher."

    08

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    So, should your friend open the shop?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then the reasons, the risks, and what she should check next.

    Good answer

    "Yes, it's a reasonable idea. The market is about 7 million cups a year, and she'd need about 205,000 to break even, roughly 3 percent of the market, which looks achievable."

    Better answer

    "Yes, but she should plan to earn back her opening cost over about two years, not one. Breaking even in year one, including the opening cost, means about 560 cups a day, a very busy shop. Covering just the running costs takes about 225 a day, and at about 400 a day the opening cost pays back in about two years. So how long she plans to run the shop really matters."

    "To get there, she needs a strong location near the university, a loyalty card for regulars, and a space people want to stay in. Before signing a lease, she should check who she's up against: one dominant chain would make it hard to win share, but lots of small shops are easier to beat."

    Bain's better answer makes the same points: think about how long it takes to earn back the opening cost, and about go-to-market, standing out, and the shape of the competition. The cups-per-day check is an extra that makes the answer feel real.

    09

    Bonus drills: bottom-up and payback

    Two short reps that turn the good answer into the better one.

    Bottom-up: how many cups can one shop sell?

    Interviewer

    Check your answer from the other direction. Roughly how many cups could one busy shop sell in a day?

    Your move

    Split the day into busy and quiet hours, and estimate cups per hour for each.

    1. Morning rush3 hours × 60 cups an hour180
    2. Rest of the day9 hours × 25 cups an hour225
    3. Per day180 + 225≈400
    4. Per year400 × 365≈146,000
    5. CompareBelow 204,675 (year one), above 81,870 (running costs)✓
    6. So whatYear-one breakeven is a stretch; plan on about 2 years✓

    Interview-style assumptions, not industry data.

    Payback: how long to earn back the opening cost?

    Interviewer

    If she sells about 400 cups a day, how long until she earns back the opening cost?

    Your move

    Work out yearly profit after running costs, then divide the opening cost by it.

    Strong answer

    Payback = opening cost ÷ yearly profit
    • Cups a year: 400 × 365 ≈ 146,000.
    • Margin: 146,000 × £2 ≈ £292,000.
    • Profit after running costs: £292,000 − £163,740 ≈ £128,000.
    • Payback: £245,610 ÷ £128,000 ≈ 1.9 years.

    "At about 400 cups a day, she earns back the opening cost in just under two years. If she plans to run the shop for five years, that's a good investment, as long as she can reach that volume."
